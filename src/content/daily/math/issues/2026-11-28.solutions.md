**1.** Both sides are false in exactly one situation: $P$ and $Q$ true, $R$ false.

| $P$ | $Q$ | $R$ | $Q \to R$ | $P \to (Q \to R)$ | $P \land Q$ | $(P \land Q) \to R$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| T | T | T | T | T | T | T |
| T | T | F | F | **F** | T | **F** |
| T | F | T | T | T | F | T |
| T | F | F | T | T | F | T |
| F | T | T | T | T | F | T |
| F | T | F | F | T | F | T |
| F | F | T | T | T | F | T |
| F | F | F | T | T | F | T |

The two columns agree in every row, so the biconditional is a **tautology**. In words: "if it's Saturday, then if it's sunny I'll go hiking" says the same as "if it's Saturday *and* sunny, I'll go hiking". Programmers know this as *currying*: a function of two arguments is the same as a function returning a function (week 10 of The Daily Commit).

**2.** Formalise: $\forall s\; \exists p\; \big(\text{solved}(s,p) \land \forall t \ne s\; \lnot\text{solved}(t,p)\big)$. Negate, flipping each quantifier and applying De Morgan:

$$
\exists s\; \forall p\; \big(\lnot\text{solved}(s,p) \lor \exists t \ne s\; \text{solved}(t,p)\big).
$$

In words: "**Some student has the property that every problem is either one they didn't solve, or one someone else also solved.**" More naturally: some student solved no problem on their own.

**3.** A subset is a yes/no choice for each of the 4 elements. Fixing "1: yes" leaves 3 free choices, so there are $2^3 = $ **8** subsets containing 1. Fixing "1: yes, 2: no" leaves 2 free choices: $2^2 = $ **4**. (Exactly half the subsets contain any given element, which makes sense: the choice for it is a coin flip.)

**4.** *Reflexive:* $x - x = 0 \in \mathbb{Z}$. *Symmetric:* if $x - y = k \in \mathbb{Z}$, then $y - x = -k \in \mathbb{Z}$. *Transitive:* if $x - y = k$ and $y - z = h$, then $x - z = k + h \in \mathbb{Z}$. $\blacksquare$

The class of $3.7$ is $\{\dots, -0.3, 0.7, 1.7, 2.7, 3.7, \dots\} = \{0.7 + k : k \in \mathbb{Z}\}$: all numbers with **fractional part 0.7**. Each class has exactly one representative in $[0, 1)$. Geometrically, gluing together points that differ by an integer **wraps the real line around a circle** of circumference 1: walking one unit brings you back to where you started. That circle is how angles work (week 17), and it's why $e^{2\pi i x}$ only depends on the class of $x$ (week 41).

**5.** *Proof by cases.* If $n = 2k$: $n^2 + n = 4k^2 + 2k = 2(2k^2 + k)$, even. If $n = 2k+1$: $n^2 + n = 4k^2 + 4k + 1 + 2k + 1 = 2(2k^2 + 3k + 1)$, even.

*Proof by factorisation.* $n^2 + n = n(n+1)$, a product of two consecutive integers. One of any two consecutive integers is even, so the product is even. $\blacksquare$

The second proof is shorter *and* explains why. Good proofs often come from finding the right way to look at the expression.

**6.** Suppose, for contradiction, that $a + b$ is rational; call it $c$. Then $b = c - a$ is a difference of two rationals, hence rational. That contradicts $b$ being irrational. So **$a + b$ is irrational**. $\blacksquare$

**7. ★** The formulas and their negations:

| Property | Formula | Negation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| reflexive | $\forall a\; aRa$ | $\exists a\; \lnot(aRa)$ |
| symmetric | $\forall a, b\; (aRb \to bRa)$ | $\exists a, b\; (aRb \land \lnot bRa)$ |
| transitive | $\forall a, b, c\; (aRb \land bRc \to aRc)$ | $\exists a,b,c\; (aRb \land bRc \land \lnot aRc)$ |

Example: $R = \{(1,1), (1,2), (2,1), (2,2)\}$ on $\{1, 2, 3\}$. It's symmetric (mirror image across the diagonal) and transitive (it's "everything" on $\{1,2\}$). But $(3,3) \notin R$, so the negation of reflexivity holds with witness $a = 3$.

*The flaw:* "Pick $b$ with $aRb$" assumes such a $b$ **exists**. For $a = 3$ above, nothing is related to 3. The proof only shows: *if $a$ is related to something, then $aRa$.* Hidden existence assumptions are among the most common mistakes in proofs. Whenever you write "pick", ask: am I sure there's something to pick?
