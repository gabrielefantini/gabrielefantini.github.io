## Sentences with a blank

Yesterday we met propositions: sentences that are true or false. But most interesting mathematical statements have a blank in them:

$$
P(x):\quad x^2 > x
$$

On its own, $P(x)$ isn't true or false. It's a **predicate**: a machine that takes an $x$ and returns a proposition. $P(3)$ is "$9 > 3$", true. $P(\tfrac12)$ is "$\tfrac14 > \tfrac12$", false. $P(1)$ is "$1 > 1$", false.

There are two ways to turn a predicate into a single proposition without choosing one $x$:

- **Universal quantifier**, $\forall x\; P(x)$: "**for every** $x$, $P(x)$ holds."
- **Existential quantifier**, $\exists x\; P(x)$: "**there exists** (at least one) $x$ such that $P(x)$ holds."

Every quantifier needs a **domain**, the set that $x$ ranges over. "$\forall x\; x^2 \ge 0$" is true if $x$ ranges over the real numbers and false over the complex numbers ($i^2 = -1$). Mathematicians write the domain into the quantifier: $\forall x \in \mathbb{R}$, $\exists n \in \mathbb{N}$.

> **The big picture.** Think of $\forall$ as a giant "and", and $\exists$ as a giant "or". Over the domain $\{1, 2, 3\}$:
> $\forall x\, P(x)$ means $P(1) \land P(2) \land P(3)$, and $\exists x\, P(x)$ means $P(1) \lor P(2) \lor P(3)$.

That one idea explains almost everything that follows.

Read the quantifiers **from left to right**, like a game:

- $\forall x\; \exists y\; L(x, y)$: "for every $x$, there is a $y$ that $x$ loves." Whoever you pick, *they* get to choose someone. **Every row has at least one dot.** Different people may love different people.
- $\exists x\; \forall y\; L(x, y)$: "there's an $x$ who loves every $y$." **Some row is completely full.**

These are different statements. The left grid satisfies the first but not the second: no row is full. And the right grid satisfies the second but not the first: one full row says nothing about the *other* rows, which can be empty. What a full row does guarantee is $\forall y\, \exists x\, L(x,y)$: every **column** has a dot, because that one row puts a dot in each column.

The lesson: **swapping $\forall$ and $\exists$ changes the meaning.** Same-type quantifiers *can* be swapped: $\forall x\, \forall y$ is the same as $\forall y\, \forall x$ (the whole grid is full).

This isn't just a puzzle. The definition of a continuous function, which you'll meet in week 30, is "$\forall \varepsilon\; \exists \delta \ldots$". Swap the quantifiers and you get a *different* definition: uniform continuity.

## Negating without thinking

How do you deny "every swan is white"? Not by saying "every swan is non-white". You only need **one** black swan:

$$
\lnot\, \forall x\; P(x) \;\equiv\; \exists x\; \lnot P(x)
$$

And to deny "there's a unicorn in this room", you must check that *every* animal in the room isn't one:

$$
\lnot\, \exists x\; P(x) \;\equiv\; \forall x\; \lnot P(x)
$$

These are De Morgan's laws again, from yesterday's exercise 5, because $\forall$ is a big "and" and $\exists$ is a big "or". Negating an "and" of many things gives an "or" of their negations.

The rule for long statements is mechanical: **push the $\lnot$ from left to right; each quantifier it passes through flips ($\forall \leftrightarrow \exists$); when it reaches the predicate, negate the predicate.**

Let's use it on something real. A function $f$ is *bounded* if

$$
\exists M \in \mathbb{R}\;\; \forall x \in \mathbb{R}:\;\; |f(x)| \le M.
$$

In words: there's a ceiling $M$ that the graph never goes above (in absolute value). What does "$f$ is **not** bounded" mean? Push the negation through:

$$
\lnot\,\exists M\; \forall x\; |f(x)| \le M
\;\equiv\; \forall M\; \lnot\,\forall x\; |f(x)| \le M
\;\equiv\; \forall M\; \exists x\; |f(x)| > M.
$$

"Whatever ceiling you propose, I can find a point that breaks through it." That's exactly what unbounded should mean, and we got it without thinking.

### Negating implications

One more ingredient. From yesterday, $P \to Q \equiv \lnot P \lor Q$, so

$$
\lnot (P \to Q) \;\equiv\; P \land \lnot Q.
$$

The negation of "if it rains, the ground is wet" is not "if it rains, the ground is dry". It's "it rained **and** the ground is dry": the broken promise. Combined with quantifiers: the negation of "every prime is odd", $\forall n\,(\text{prime}(n) \to \text{odd}(n))$, is $\exists n\,(\text{prime}(n) \land \lnot\text{odd}(n))$, "some prime is even". And there is one: $n = 2$.

> **Why it matters.** Every proof by contradiction starts by negating a statement, and every counterexample is a witness for an $\exists$. You'll negate quantified statements hundreds of times over the next two years. Practise it now and it becomes automatic.

**2.** Write in symbols, with the domain stated: (a) "Every natural number has a successor." (b) "There's a smallest natural number." (c) "No real number squares to $-1$."

**3.** Negate each statement and simplify so that $\lnot$ only appears right next to a predicate (or is absorbed: $\lnot(a < b)$ becomes $a \ge b$): (a) $\forall x\; \exists y\; x + y = 0$; (b) $\exists x\; \forall y\; xy = y$; (c) $\forall \varepsilon > 0\; \exists N\; \forall n \ge N\; |a_n| < \varepsilon$.

**4.** Over the domain $\{1, 2, 3\}$, write $\exists x\, \forall y\, (x \le y)$ without quantifiers, using only $\land$ and $\lor$. Is it true?

**5.** Give an example of a predicate $L(x,y)$ on a domain of 3 people where $\forall y\, \exists x\, L(x,y)$ is true but $\exists x\, \forall y\, L(x,y)$ is false. Draw its grid.
