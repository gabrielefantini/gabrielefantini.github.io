## When order matters

Yesterday's sets ignore order: $\{1, 2\} = \{2, 1\}$. But a point on a map, a date (day, month), or a chess square (file, rank) needs order: $(1, 2)$ and $(2, 1)$ are different points. So we introduce the **ordered pair** $(a, b)$, with the one rule that matters:

$$
(a, b) = (c, d) \iff a = c \text{ and } b = d.
$$

The **Cartesian product** of two sets is the set of all ordered pairs you can make from them:

$$
A \times B = \{(a, b) : a \in A,\ b \in B\}.
$$

The name comes from René Descartes, and so does the picture. If $A = \{1, 2, 3\}$ and $B = \{x, y\}$, lay $A$ along one axis and $B$ along the other. The product is the **grid of all crossings**:

Two things fall out of the picture:

- **Counting:** $|A \times B| = |A| \cdot |B|$. Each row has $|A|$ points, and there are $|B|$ rows. That's why it's called a *product*.
- **The plane:** $\mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{R} = \mathbb{R}^2$ is the set of all points with two real coordinates. Analytic geometry (weeks 10–13) is the study of subsets of this product.

Order matters, so in general $A \times B \neq B \times A$. Swapping turns the grid on its side.

## A relation is a set of dots

Here's an idea that's almost too simple. A **relation** from $A$ to $B$ is *any subset* $R \subseteq A \times B$. If $(a, b) \in R$ we write $a \mathrel{R} b$ and say "$a$ is related to $b$".

That's the whole definition. On the grid, a relation is just **a choice of which dots to colour**. On Tuesday you already drew one: "$x$ loves $y$" was a set of dots on the people × people grid.

Familiar relations are subsets of $\mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{R}$ too:

- "$x = y$" is the diagonal line.
- "$x < y$" is the half-plane above the diagonal.
- "$x^2 + y^2 = 1$" is the unit circle.

So the graph of an equation *is* a relation, drawn.

### Three properties worth naming

Most interesting relations go from a set to itself ($R \subseteq A \times A$). For these, three properties come up again and again. Each one is a statement about the picture:

| Property | Definition | On the grid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Reflexive** | $\forall a\;\; a \mathrel R a$ | the whole diagonal is coloured |
| **Symmetric** | $\forall a, b\;\; a \mathrel R b \to b \mathrel R a$ | the picture is a mirror image across the diagonal |
| **Transitive** | $\forall a, b, c\;\; (a \mathrel R b \land b \mathrel R c) \to a \mathrel R c$ | you can "chain" steps |

Check them on familiar examples over $\mathbb{Z}$:

- $=$ is reflexive, symmetric and transitive.
- $\le$ is reflexive and transitive, but not symmetric ($1 \le 2$ but $2 \not\le 1$).
- $<$ is transitive only. It's not reflexive, because $1 < 1$ is false.
- "differ by exactly 1" ($|a - b| = 1$) is symmetric only. It's not transitive: $1 \to 2 \to 3$, but $|1 - 3| = 2$.

## Same remainder, same box

A relation that is reflexive, symmetric **and** transitive is called an **equivalence relation**. It's a way of saying "these things are the same *for my purposes*."

The great example: on $\mathbb{Z}$, say $a \sim b$ if $a$ and $b$ leave the same remainder when divided by 3. Equivalently, $3$ divides $a - b$. Check the three properties:

- **Reflexive:** $a - a = 0$, and $3$ divides $0$.
- **Symmetric:** if $a - b = 3k$, then $b - a = 3(-k)$.
- **Transitive:** if $a - b = 3k$ and $b - c = 3h$, then $a - c = (a - b) + (b - c) = 3(k + h)$.

Now here's the magic. An equivalence relation **sorts the set into boxes**. Every integer lands in exactly one of three boxes:

$$
\{\dots, -3, 0, 3, 6, \dots\}, \quad \{\dots, -2, 1, 4, 7, \dots\}, \quad \{\dots, -1, 2, 5, 8, \dots\}.
$$

Inside a box everyone is related to everyone. Across boxes, nobody is. These boxes are called **equivalence classes**, and the collection of boxes is a **partition**: the boxes don't overlap, and together they cover everything.

> **The big picture.** An equivalence relation and a partition into boxes are *the same thing*, seen from two sides. Give me the boxes, and I'll define "related" as "in the same box". Give me an equivalence relation, and the boxes are the classes. This is how mathematicians build new objects. A clock is $\mathbb{Z}$ with numbers 12 apart glued together. A fraction $\tfrac{1}{2}$ is the box $\{(1,2), (2,4), (3,6), \dots\}$.

### Functions are relations too

One last connection. A **function** $f: A \to B$ is a relation from $A$ to $B$ in which **every $a \in A$ is related to exactly one $b \in B$**. On the grid: every column has exactly one dot. That dot is at height $f(a)$, and the set of dots is the *graph* of $f$. We'll spend all of week 9 on functions. For now, notice that you already know what one is: a special set of dots on a grid.

> **Why it matters.** Relations are the "R" in *relational databases* (week 40 of The Daily Commit): a database table is literally a subset of a Cartesian product $\text{Col}_1 \times \text{Col}_2 \times \cdots$. And arithmetic modulo $n$ (week 60) runs every hash table and every cryptographic protocol you use.

## Exercises

**1.** Let $A = \{0, 1\}$ and $B = \{a, b, c\}$. List all elements of $A \times B$ and of $A \times A$. How many relations from $A$ to $B$ are there?

**2.** For each relation on $\{1, 2, 3\}$, say whether it's reflexive, symmetric, transitive: (a) $R_1 = \{(1,1), (2,2), (3,3), (1,2), (2,1)\}$; (b) $R_2 = \{(1,2), (2,3)\}$; (c) $R_3 = \varnothing$; (d) $R_4 = \{1,2,3\} \times \{1,2,3\}$.

**3.** On the set of all people, which of these are equivalence relations? (a) "was born in the same year as"; (b) "is a sibling of"; (c) "is at least as tall as".

**4.** On $\mathbb{Z}$, define $a \sim b$ iff $a - b$ is even. Prove it's an equivalence relation and describe its classes.

**5.** Sketch the relation $\{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2 : x^2 + y^2 \le 4 \text{ and } y \ge x\}$ and decide whether it's a function from $\mathbb{R}$ to $\mathbb{R}$.

**6. ★** On $\mathbb{Z} \times (\mathbb{Z} \setminus \{0\})$ (pairs of integers whose second element isn't zero), define $(a, b) \sim (c, d)$ iff $ad = bc$. Prove it's an equivalence relation. Transitivity is the tricky part: where do you need $b, d, f \neq 0$? What are the classes?
