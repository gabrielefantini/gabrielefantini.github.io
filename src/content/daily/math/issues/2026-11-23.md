## A sentence you can bet on

Here's a puzzle to start with. Which of these can you bet money on?

1. "7 is a prime number."
2. "Close the door."
3. "This number is even."
4. "There are infinitely many primes."

You can bet on (1): it's true. You can bet on (4): it's also true, though proving it takes some work (we'll do it in week 2). You can't bet on (2), because an order is neither true nor false. And you can't bet on (3) until someone tells you *which* number they mean.

That's the whole definition. A **proposition** is a statement that is either true or false, never both. Sentences like (3), which become propositions once you fill in a blank, are called **predicates**. Tomorrow's issue is about them.

We name propositions with letters: $P$ = "7 is prime", $Q$ = "7 is even". Then we stop caring what they say, and only care about their **truth value**: $\mathsf{T}$ or $\mathsf{F}$ (or 1 and 0, if you think like a computer). The mathematics happens when we combine them.

## The five connectives

There are five ways of gluing propositions together that you'll use constantly:

| Symbol | Name | Read as | True exactly when… |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\lnot P$ | negation | "not $P$" | $P$ is false |
| $P \land Q$ | conjunction | "$P$ and $Q$" | both are true |
| $P \lor Q$ | disjunction | "$P$ or $Q$" | at least one is true |
| $P \to Q$ | implication | "if $P$ then $Q$" | it's *not* the case that $P$ is true and $Q$ false |
| $P \leftrightarrow Q$ | biconditional | "$P$ if and only if $Q$" | $P$ and $Q$ have the same value |

Two of these need a closer look, because everyday language gets them slightly wrong.

**"Or" is inclusive.** In mathematics, "$P$ or $Q$" is true when both are true. "You can have cake or ice cream" in a restaurant usually means *one of them*. That's a different connective, *exclusive or* (XOR), written $P \oplus Q$.

**"If… then" is a promise.** This is the one that confuses everyone at first. Look at its truth table:

| $P$ | $Q$ | $P \to Q$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| T | T | T |
| T | F | **F** |
| F | T | T |
| F | F | T |

Why on earth is "if $P$ then $Q$" *true* when $P$ is false?

Think of it as a promise. A parent says: "**If** you pass the exam, **then** I'll buy you a bike." When have they broken the promise? Only in one situation: you passed, and there's no bike. If you failed the exam, the promise simply wasn't triggered. Whether a bike shows up or not, nobody can call the parent a liar.

> **The big picture.** $P \to Q$ doesn't say that $P$ *causes* $Q$, or that $P$ is true. It says just one thing: "the situation *$P$ true and $Q$ false* never happens." Everything else is allowed.

This is why mathematicians happily say "if $2 + 2 = 5$, then I am the Pope" is true. The promise is never put to the test.

Now look at the picture rather than the formulas:

- $P \land Q$ colours **one** cell. $P \lor Q$ colours **three**: every cell except FF.
- $P \to Q$ is "everything except the broken promise".
- $P \leftrightarrow Q$ colours the diagonal: the cells where $P$ and $Q$ agree.

Here's the surprise. A connective of two propositions is *nothing more* than a choice of which of the four cells to colour. Each cell is coloured or not, independently, so there are exactly

$$
2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 2 = 2^4 = 16
$$

possible connectives. That's all of them. Our five familiar ones (counting $\lnot P$, which ignores $Q$ and colours a column) are just the useful members of a family of sixteen. XOR is there too: it colours the anti-diagonal.

### Building bigger truth tables

With three propositions $P, Q, R$ there are $2^3 = 8$ rows. To evaluate something like $(P \lor Q) \to \lnot R$, add one column per sub-formula, from the inside out:

| $P$ | $Q$ | $R$ | $P \lor Q$ | $\lnot R$ | $(P \lor Q) \to \lnot R$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| T | T | T | T | F | F |
| T | T | F | T | T | T |
| T | F | T | T | F | F |
| T | F | F | T | T | T |
| F | T | T | T | F | F |
| F | T | F | T | T | T |
| F | F | T | F | F | T |
| F | F | F | F | T | T |

This is completely mechanical, and that's the point. A computer can do it, which is why truth tables are the first bridge between mathematics and software. Every `if` statement you'll ever write is evaluating one of these.

### Tautologies and equivalence

Two special situations deserve names:

- A **tautology** is true in every row, like $P \lor \lnot P$ ("it's raining or it isn't"). A **contradiction** is false in every row, like $P \land \lnot P$.
- Two formulas are **logically equivalent**, written $A \equiv B$, when they have the same truth value in every row. In pictures: *they colour the same cells.*

A first equivalence worth memorising:

$$
P \to Q \;\equiv\; \lnot P \lor Q
$$

Check it with the grid. $\lnot P$ colours the right column ($P$ false), $Q$ colours the top row, and their union is every cell except bottom-left. That's exactly the picture of $P \to Q$.

> **Why it matters.** Logical equivalence is how you simplify conditions in code, how chip designers shrink circuits (week 12 of The Daily Commit), and the basis of every proof technique we'll meet on Friday.

## Exercises

**1.** Which of these are propositions? For those that are, give their truth value. (a) "$12$ is divisible by $5$." (b) "Is it raining?" (c) "$x + 1 = 3$." (d) "Every even number greater than 2 is the sum of two primes."

**2.** Let $P$ = "it's cold" and $Q$ = "it's snowing". Translate into symbols: (a) "It's cold but not snowing." (b) "If it's snowing, then it's cold." (c) "It's snowing only if it's cold." (d) "It's neither cold nor snowing."

**3.** Write the truth table of $(P \to Q) \land (Q \to P)$ and compare it with $P \leftrightarrow Q$. What do you notice?

**4.** Using truth tables, decide which of these are tautologies: (a) $P \to (P \lor Q)$; (b) $(P \to Q) \to (Q \to P)$; (c) $\big((P \to Q) \land P\big) \to Q$.

**5.** Show that $\lnot(P \land Q) \equiv \lnot P \lor \lnot Q$, first with a truth table and then with the $2 \times 2$ grid picture.

**6. ★** We said there are 16 connectives of two propositions. Show that **all 16** can be written using only $\lnot$ and $\land$. (Hint: first write $\lor$ using only $\lnot$ and $\land$. Then think about how to describe "exactly this set of cells".)
