## Why bother proving things?

Check this claim: *$n^2 + n + 41$ is prime for every natural number $n$.*

Try it. $n = 0$: 41, prime. $n = 1$: 43, prime. $n = 2$: 47. $n = 3$: 53. Keep going and you'll find primes for $n = 0, 1, 2, \dots, 39$, forty in a row. Surely that's enough evidence?

It isn't. At $n = 40$ you get $40^2 + 40 + 41 = 40 \cdot 41 + 41 = 41 \cdot 41$, which is not prime.

This is why mathematicians prove things. Examples can make you confident, but only a proof makes you *certain*. A proof covers infinitely many cases with a finite argument.

Every proof we'll write this week has the same skeleton, built from Monday's logic: we want to show $P \to Q$, usually for all $x$ in some domain. There are three basic strategies.

## Strategy 1: direct proof

**Assume $P$. Follow the logic. Arrive at $Q$.**

**Claim.** The sum of two odd integers is even.

First, translate the words into algebra. "Odd" means "of the form $2k + 1$ for some integer $k$". "Even" means "of the form $2m$".

*Proof.* Let $a$ and $b$ be odd. Then $a = 2k + 1$ and $b = 2h + 1$ for some integers $k, h$. Notice that we used **two different letters**. Using $k$ for both would secretly assume $a = b$. Then

$$
a + b = 2k + 1 + 2h + 1 = 2(k + h + 1),
$$

and $k + h + 1$ is an integer, so $a + b$ is even. $\blacksquare$

That's the pattern. **Unpack the definitions, compute, repack into the definition you want.** Most proofs in the first year are exactly this.

## Strategy 2: the contrapositive

Sometimes the direct road is blocked. Try this one directly:

**Claim.** If $n^2$ is even, then $n$ is even.

Assume $n^2 = 2m$. Then $n = \sqrt{2m}$… and we're stuck. Square roots don't help.

Here's the trick. Back on Monday, the implication $P \to Q$ was false in only one cell of the grid: $P$ true, $Q$ false. Now look at the **contrapositive** $\lnot Q \to \lnot P$. When is *it* false? When $\lnot Q$ is true and $\lnot P$ is false, which is again $Q$ false and $P$ true. **The same cell.**

$$
P \to Q \;\equiv\; \lnot Q \to \lnot P.
$$

So we can prove the contrapositive instead: **if $n$ is odd, then $n^2$ is odd.** Now the road is open:

*Proof.* Suppose $n$ is odd, $n = 2k + 1$. Then $n^2 = 4k^2 + 4k + 1 = 2(2k^2 + 2k) + 1$, which is odd. By contraposition, if $n^2$ is even then $n$ is even. $\blacksquare$

> **The big picture.** "If it rained, the ground is wet" and "if the ground is dry, it didn't rain" are the same promise, seen from the other side. When the hypothesis is awkward (like "$n^2$ is even") and its negation is easy to use (like "$n$ is odd", which gives you a formula), flip the implication.

Be careful not to confuse the contrapositive with the **converse**, $Q \to P$. The converse is a *different* statement, and on Monday (exercise 4b) we saw it can be false when the original is true.

## Strategy 3: contradiction

**To prove $Q$, assume $\lnot Q$, and derive something impossible.** If assuming the opposite leads to nonsense, then the opposite is false.

This is the right tool for statements that say something *doesn't* exist.

**Claim.** There is no smallest positive rational number.

*Proof.* Suppose, for contradiction, that there is one; call it $r$. So $r > 0$, $r \in \mathbb{Q}$, and $r \le q$ for every positive rational $q$. Now consider $\tfrac{r}{2}$. It's rational (a rational divided by 2), and it's positive. So by our assumption $r \le \tfrac r2$. Multiplying by $\tfrac2r > 0$ gives $2 \le 1$. That's impossible. So our assumption was false, and no smallest positive rational exists. $\blacksquare$

The structure is always the same:

1. Say clearly what you're assuming: "Suppose, for contradiction, that…" (this is where Tuesday's negation rules come in).
2. Reason correctly from it.
3. Reach a contradiction, something of the form $R \land \lnot R$.
4. Conclude that the assumption was false.

Next week (week 2) we'll use exactly this method for the most famous proof of all: $\sqrt 2$ is not a fraction.

## Disproof: one counterexample is enough

How do you show a "for all" statement is **false**? By Tuesday's rule, $\lnot\,\forall x\, P(x) \equiv \exists x\, \lnot P(x)$. You need **one** $x$ that fails, a **counterexample**. You don't need a general argument.

"$n^2 + n + 41$ is always prime" is destroyed by $n = 40$. "Every continuous function is differentiable" is destroyed by $f(x) = |x|$ at $0$ (week 31). One example is a complete disproof.

The asymmetry is worth remembering:

| To prove… | you need… |
| --- | --- |
| $\forall x\, P(x)$ | a general argument (examples prove nothing) |
| $\exists x\, P(x)$ | one example (a "witness") |
| $\lnot\,\forall x\, P(x)$ | one counterexample |
| $\lnot\,\exists x\, P(x)$ | a general argument |

> **Why it matters.** Proof is the core skill of the whole programme. In software it has a direct twin: a test case is an example (it can show a bug, never prove there are none), while a **loop invariant** argument (week 58) or a type system (week 95 of The Daily Commit) gives guarantees for all inputs.

## Exercises

**1.** Prove directly: the product of two odd integers is odd.

**2.** Prove directly: if $a$ divides $b$ and $b$ divides $c$, then $a$ divides $c$. ("$a$ divides $b$", written $a \mid b$, means $b = ak$ for some integer $k$.)

**3.** Write the contrapositive and the converse of: "If a quadrilateral is a square, then its diagonals are equal." Which are true?

**4.** Prove by contraposition: if $n^2$ is odd, then $n$ is odd. Then prove: if $3 \nmid n$, then $3 \nmid n^2$. (Hint: $n = 3k + 1$ or $n = 3k + 2$.)

**5.** Find a counterexample to each: (a) "If $a^2 = b^2$ then $a = b$" (over $\mathbb{R}$); (b) "Every prime is odd"; (c) "$2^n > n^2$ for every $n \ge 1$".

**6. ★** Prove by contradiction that there are no integers $a, b$ with $4a + 6b = 1$. Then generalise: for which integers $c$ does $4a + 6b = c$ have integer solutions? (You'll get the full answer, Bézout's identity, in week 60. Try to guess it now.)
