**1.** (a) A proposition, **false**: $12 = 2 \cdot 5 + 2$. (b) Not a proposition: it's a question. (c) Not a proposition: its truth depends on $x$ (it's a *predicate*, true only for $x = 2$). (d) A proposition: it's either true or false, even though nobody knows which yet. This is Goldbach's conjecture, checked by computer up to $4 \cdot 10^{18}$ but never proved. Being a proposition is about having *a* truth value, not about knowing it.

**2.** (a) "But" means "and" in logic: $P \land \lnot Q$. (b) $Q \to P$. (c) "$A$ only if $B$" means "$A$ can't happen without $B$", i.e. $A \to B$. So "snowing only if cold" is $Q \to P$, the same as (b). (d) "Neither… nor" is "not this and not that": $\lnot P \land \lnot Q$.

**3.**

| $P$ | $Q$ | $P \to Q$ | $Q \to P$ | $(P\to Q)\land(Q\to P)$ | $P \leftrightarrow Q$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| T | T | T | T | T | T |
| T | F | F | T | F | F |
| F | T | T | F | F | F |
| F | F | T | T | T | T |

The last two columns are identical, so $(P\to Q)\land(Q\to P) \equiv P \leftrightarrow Q$. This is why "if and only if" is called the *bi*conditional: it's an implication in both directions. To prove an "iff" statement, you prove two implications.

**4.** (a) $P \to (P \lor Q)$: the only way an implication fails is "hypothesis true, conclusion false". If $P$ is true, then $P \lor Q$ is true too, so it never fails. **Tautology.**

(b) $(P \to Q) \to (Q \to P)$: take $P = \mathsf F$, $Q = \mathsf T$. Then $P \to Q$ is T but $Q \to P$ is F, so the whole thing is F. **Not a tautology.** Swapping the two sides of an implication (taking its *converse*) can change its truth: "if it's a square, it's a rectangle" is true, "if it's a rectangle, it's a square" isn't.

(c) $\big((P \to Q) \land P\big) \to Q$: suppose the hypothesis is true. Then $P$ is T and $P \to Q$ is T, which forces $Q$ to be T. So the conclusion is true whenever the hypothesis is. **Tautology.** This one has a name, *modus ponens*, and it's the basic step of every proof: "$P$ implies $Q$; $P$ is true; therefore $Q$."

**5.** Truth table:

| $P$ | $Q$ | $P \land Q$ | $\lnot(P \land Q)$ | $\lnot P$ | $\lnot Q$ | $\lnot P \lor \lnot Q$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| T | T | T | **F** | F | F | **F** |
| T | F | F | **T** | F | T | **T** |
| F | T | F | **T** | T | F | **T** |
| F | F | F | **T** | T | T | **T** |

The bold columns match. With the grid: $P \land Q$ colours only the top-left cell, so its negation colours the other three. $\lnot P$ colours the right column and $\lnot Q$ the bottom row, and their union is also every cell except top-left. Same picture, so the formulas are equivalent. This is **De Morgan's law**, and its twin $\lnot(P \lor Q) \equiv \lnot P \land \lnot Q$ works the same way.

**6. ★** First, $\lor$: by De Morgan, $P \lor Q \equiv \lnot(\lnot P \land \lnot Q)$. So anything that can be written with $\lnot, \land, \lor$ can be written with $\lnot, \land$ alone.

Now take any of the 16 grid colourings. Each single cell can be described by an "and" of two literals:

- top-left ($P$ T, $Q$ T): $P \land Q$
- bottom-left ($P$ T, $Q$ F): $P \land \lnot Q$
- top-right ($P$ F, $Q$ T): $\lnot P \land Q$
- bottom-right ($P$ F, $Q$ F): $\lnot P \land \lnot Q$

A colouring with several cells is the "or" of the formulas for its cells. For example XOR is $(P \land \lnot Q) \lor (\lnot P \land Q)$. The colouring with no cells is the contradiction $P \land \lnot P$. So every connective can be written with $\lnot, \land, \lor$, and by the first step with $\lnot, \land$ only. $\blacksquare$

This "or of ands" form is called the *disjunctive normal form* (week 57). The same idea is why a whole computer can be built from a single kind of gate, NAND, as you'll see in week 12 of The Daily Commit.
